A \emph{free-body diagram} is a diagram in which a chosen body or system is
isolated from its surroundings and every relevant \emph{external} force acting on
that body or system is represented by a vector.  The free-body diagram is the
principal bridge between a physical description and Newton's equations of motion.

The important word in the definition is \emph{chosen}.  Before drawing force
arrows, one must decide what object or collection of objects is the system.  A
force is external or internal only relative to that system boundary.  A force that
must appear when one object is isolated may disappear from the equations when two
interacting objects are treated as one larger system.

For a particle or translating rigid body of constant mass, the free-body diagram
is converted into the vector equation
\[
\sum \mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}=m\mathbf a.
\]
For a rigid body, the same diagram also supplies the forces and moment arms used in
rotational dynamics,
\[
\sum \boldsymbol\tau_O
=\frac{d\mathbf L_O}{dt},
\]
or, in the appropriate fixed-axis special case,
\[
\sum \tau=I\alpha.
\]
Thus a good free-body diagram is not decorative; it is a compact statement of the
physical model.

\section*{System boundary}

The first step in a mechanics problem is to state what is being modeled.  A
\emph{system boundary} separates the chosen system from everything else.  Forces
exerted across the boundary by the surroundings are external forces and belong on
the free-body diagram.  Forces between parts of the chosen system are internal and
normally do not appear on a free-body diagram of the whole system.

For example, consider two blocks in contact on a horizontal table.  If only the
left block is selected, the contact force exerted by the right block must appear.
If both blocks are selected as one system, the mutual contact forces are internal
and do not appear in the system free-body diagram.

This observation is one of the most useful problem-solving tools in mechanics:
choose a system boundary that removes forces that are not needed for the desired
quantity.

\section*{A seven-step construction procedure}

A reliable free-body diagram can be constructed by the following sequence.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Choose the system.} State explicitly whether the system is one
particle, one rigid body, or several bodies treated together.

\item \emph{Sketch the isolated body or replace it by a point.} For particle
translation, a point or simple box is usually sufficient.  Preserve the actual
shape when lines of action and torques matter.

\item \emph{Identify every interaction crossing the system boundary.} Typical
interactions are gravity, contact with a surface, strings or cables, springs,
fluids, and externally applied pushes or pulls.

\item \emph{Replace each interaction by a force vector.} Draw the force on the
chosen system, not the force exerted by the chosen system on something else.

\item \emph{Label the forces by physical origin.} Labels such as \(m\mathbf g\),
\(\mathbf N\), \(\mathbf T\), \(\mathbf f\), and \(\mathbf F_s\) are better than
labels such as ``up'' or ``left'' because the former identify interactions.

\item \emph{Choose coordinate axes.} Axes should simplify the component equations.
On an incline, axes parallel and perpendicular to the surface are usually better
than horizontal and vertical axes.

\item \emph{Write Newton's equations only after the diagram is complete.} The
component equations should be a translation of the diagram, not a substitute for
it.
\end{enumerate}

A useful final question is: ``For every force arrow, what object in the
surroundings exerts this force on the chosen system?''  If that question cannot be
answered, the arrow may not represent a real interaction.

\section*{Forces commonly appearing on free-body diagrams}

\subsection*{Weight}

Near the surface of the Earth, the weight of a body of mass \(m\) is
\[
\mathbf W=m\mathbf g,
\]
directed approximately toward the center of the Earth.  Weight acts whether or
not the body is in contact with a surface.

Weight should not be replaced by its components until coordinates have been
selected.  On an incline of angle \(\theta\), if the axes are parallel and
perpendicular to the plane, the components have magnitudes
\[
mg\sin\theta
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
mg\cos\theta,
\]
but these components are not additional forces.  The free-body diagram contains
one gravitational force, \(m\mathbf g\).

\subsection*{Normal force}

A normal force is a contact force perpendicular to a surface.  Its magnitude is
not automatically equal to \(mg\).  It must be determined from the equations of
motion and the geometry of the contact.

For a block on a horizontal surface with no other vertical forces and zero vertical
acceleration,
\[
N=mg.
\]
For a block on an incline with no other force normal to the plane,
\[
N=mg\cos\theta.
\]
If another applied force has a vertical component or if the body has vertical
acceleration, the normal force changes accordingly.

\subsection*{Tension}

An ideal flexible string or cable pulls along its own direction.  A tension force
on a body therefore points away from the body along the string.  In the common
ideal model of a massless string over frictionless massless pulleys, the tension
magnitude is the same throughout a continuous string.  Real ropes and pulleys do
not always satisfy this idealization.

\subsection*{Friction}

Friction acts tangentially to a contact surface and opposes relative sliding or the
\emph{tendency} to slide.  Static friction satisfies
\[
|f_s|\leq \mu_s N,
\]
whereas the common kinetic-friction model is
\[
f_k=\mu_k N.
\]

The expression \(f_s=\mu_s N\) is true only at impending slip.  In a static
problem the actual static-friction magnitude is normally found from the equations
of equilibrium and then checked against the inequality.

\subsection*{Spring force}

For an ideal linear spring,
\[
\mathbf F_s=-k\mathbf x,
\]
where \(\mathbf x\) is the displacement of the spring end from its equilibrium
position along the spring axis.  The minus sign means that the force is restoring.

\subsection*{Drag and fluid resistance}

A drag force is opposite the velocity relative to the surrounding fluid in common
isotropic models.  Two frequently used approximations are
\[
\mathbf F_d=-b\mathbf v
\]
for linear drag and
\[
\mathbf F_d=-c|\mathbf v|\mathbf v
\]
for quadratic drag.  The appropriate model depends on the flow regime and the
body.

\subsection*{Applied forces}

A push or pull supplied by an external agent may be represented by an applied
force \(\mathbf F\).  If its direction is oblique to the coordinate axes, the
vector can later be resolved into components.  Components should not be drawn in
addition to the original vector on the same free-body diagram unless the diagram
clearly states that the original vector has been replaced by its components.

\section*{Forces are not their components}

A common error is to draw both a force and its Cartesian components as if all three
were separate forces.  Suppose an applied force has magnitude \(F\) and is at
angle \(\theta\) above the positive \(x\)-axis.  One may write
\[
\mathbf F
=F\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf i}
+F\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf j}.
\]
The two component terms are a representation of \(\mathbf F\); they are not two
additional interactions.

The same warning applies to weight on an incline.  The gravitational interaction
is one force \(m\mathbf g\), even though it is often convenient to resolve it into
components parallel and perpendicular to the plane.

\section*{Newton's third law and free-body diagrams}

Newton's third law states that if body \(A\) exerts a force on body \(B\), then
body \(B\) exerts an equal and opposite force on body \(A\):
\[
\mathbf F_{A\to B}=-\mathbf F_{B\to A}.
\]
The two forces act on \emph{different bodies}.  Therefore they do not normally
appear together on the free-body diagram of either body.

For a book resting on a table, the normal force exerted by the table on the book
and the weight of the book are not a third-law pair.  Both act on the book.  The
third-law partner of the table's normal force on the book is the book's contact
force on the table.  The third-law partner of the Earth's gravitational force on
the book is the book's gravitational force on the Earth.

This distinction prevents one of the most persistent introductory-mechanics
errors: interpreting every pair of equal and opposite arrows on a single diagram
as a Newton's-third-law pair.

\section*{Choosing coordinate axes}

Coordinate axes are mathematical choices, not physical forces.  Good axes simplify
the equations.

\subsection*{Inclined surfaces}

For a block on a plane inclined at angle \(\theta\), choose the \(x\)-axis along
the plane and the \(y\)-axis normal to it.  Then weight resolves as
\[
W_x=mg\sin\theta,
\qquad
W_y=-mg\cos\theta.
\]
The normal force lies entirely along the \(y\)-axis and friction, when present,
lies along the \(x\)-axis.

\subsection*{Circular motion}

For motion on a curved path, radial--tangential coordinates are often more useful
than fixed Cartesian axes.  The radial equation contains the normal acceleration
\[
a_r=\frac{v^2}{r}
\]
directed toward the center of curvature, while the tangential equation contains
\(a_t=dv/dt\).

No separate ``centripetal force'' should be added to the free-body diagram in an
inertial frame.  The phrase centripetal force refers to the radial component of the
\emph{net real force}:
\[
\sum F_r=m\frac{v^2}{r}.
\]

\section*{Particle and rigid-body free-body diagrams}

When only translational motion matters, a body may often be represented by a point.
In rigid-body statics or dynamics, however, the location and line of action of each
force matters because torque depends on the moment arm.  A rigid-body free-body
diagram should therefore preserve enough geometry to determine
\[
\boldsymbol\tau_O=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F.
\]

Distributed loads may be replaced by equivalent resultant forces when the
resultant magnitude and line of action are known.  The replacement must preserve
both the net force and the appropriate moment for the intended calculation.

\section*{Ten essential free-body-diagram archetypes}

The following archetypes cover a large fraction of first-year mechanics problems.
The descriptions specify the force arrows that belong on the diagram.

\subsection*{1. Object resting on a horizontal surface}

For a body at rest on a horizontal table with no other forces:
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\uparrow\ N\\
\text{body}\\
\downarrow\ mg
\end{array}
\]
The vertical equation is
\[
N-mg=0.
\]

\subsection*{2. Horizontal push on a frictionless surface}

The forces are weight downward, normal force upward, and the applied force
horizontally:
\[
\sum F_x=F=ma,
\qquad
N-mg=0.
\]

\subsection*{3. Horizontal push with kinetic friction}

The horizontal forces are the applied force and kinetic friction in the opposite
direction:
\[
F-f_k=ma,
\qquad
f_k=\mu_kN.
\]

\subsection*{4. Block on a frictionless incline}

The forces are weight and the normal force.  With axes along and normal to the
plane,
\[
mg\sin\theta=ma,
\qquad
N=mg\cos\theta.
\]
The downhill acceleration is therefore
\[
a=g\sin\theta.
\]

\subsection*{5. Block on a rough incline}

The forces are weight, normal force, and friction.  If the block slides downhill,
kinetic friction points uphill and
\[
mg\sin\theta-\mu_kmg\cos\theta=ma.
\]
If the block is static, the friction magnitude is whatever is required by
equilibrium provided \(|f_s|\leq\mu_sN\).

\subsection*{6. Hanging mass}

For a mass suspended by a vertical string, the forces are tension upward and
weight downward:
\[
T-mg=ma_y.
\]
The sign of \(a_y\) depends on the chosen positive direction.

\subsection*{7. Two-mass Atwood machine}

Each mass requires its own free-body diagram unless the two masses are selected as
one system.  For an ideal string and pulley with \(m_2>m_1\),
\[
T-m_1g=m_1a,
\]
\[
m_2g-T=m_2a.
\]
Adding the equations eliminates the internal tension and gives
\[
a=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2}.
\]

\subsection*{8. Mass attached to a spring}

For horizontal motion on a frictionless surface, the horizontal force is the
spring force:
\[
-kx=m\ddot x.
\]
Weight and normal force cancel vertically.

\subsection*{9. Object moving through a fluid}

For a body falling vertically through a fluid, weight acts downward while drag
acts opposite the relative velocity.  With downward chosen positive and a linear
drag model,
\[
mg-bv=m\frac{dv}{dt}.
\]
At terminal speed, the acceleration is zero, so
\[
v_t=\frac{mg}{b}.
\]

\subsection*{10. Object in vertical circular motion}

At the bottom of a vertical circular path, the inward radial direction is upward.
If the inward forces are supplied by a normal force and weight,
\[
N-mg=m\frac{v^2}{r}.
\]
At the top of an inside loop, both weight and a positive inward normal force point
toward the center:
\[
N+mg=m\frac{v^2}{r}.
\]
The force diagram changes with position because the radial direction changes.

\section*{Worked examples}

\subsection*{Example 1: block pulled across a frictionless table}

A \(5.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block is pulled horizontally by a \(20\,\mathrm{N}\) force
on a frictionless horizontal surface.  Find the acceleration and normal force.

The free-body diagram contains three forces: \(20\,\mathrm{N}\) horizontally,
\(N\) upward, and \(mg\) downward.  Newton's equations are
\[
20=5.0a
\]
and
\[
N-5.0g=0.
\]
Hence
\[
a=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\]
and, using \(g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\),
\[
N=49\,\mathrm{N}.
\]

\subsection*{Example 2: block pulled upward at an angle}

A \(10\,\mathrm{kg}\) block on a horizontal floor is pulled by a
\(50\,\mathrm{N}\) force at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal.  The coefficient
of kinetic friction is \(\mu_k=0.20\).  Find the acceleration.

The vertical force balance determines the normal force:
\[
N+50\sin30^\circ-mg=0.
\]
Thus
\[
N=98-25=73\,\mathrm{N}.
\]
The kinetic friction is
\[
f_k=\mu_kN=14.6\,\mathrm{N}.
\]
The horizontal equation is
\[
50\cos30^\circ-14.6=10a.
\]
Therefore
\[
a\approx2.87\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
\]

This example shows why one should not assume \(N=mg\): the upward component of the
pull reduces the normal force and therefore reduces the kinetic friction.

\subsection*{Example 3: frictionless incline}

A \(3.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block slides on a frictionless plane inclined
\(25^\circ\) above the horizontal.  Choose axes parallel and perpendicular to the
plane.  The free-body diagram contains only \(mg\) and \(N\).  The equations are
\[
mg\sin25^\circ=ma
\]
and
\[
N-mg\cos25^\circ=0.
\]
Thus
\[
a=g\sin25^\circ\approx4.14\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\]
and
\[
N=mg\cos25^\circ\approx26.6\,\mathrm{N}.
\]

\subsection*{Example 4: two blocks and system selection}

Two blocks of masses \(m_1=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(m_2=3.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) are in
contact on a frictionless table.  A horizontal \(15\,\mathrm{N}\) force pushes
\(m_1\), which pushes \(m_2\).  Find the acceleration and the contact force on
\(m_2\).

First choose both blocks as one system.  The contact force is internal, so
\[
15=(m_1+m_2)a.
\]
Hence
\[
a=\frac{15}{5.0}=3.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
\]
Now isolate \(m_2\).  Its only horizontal force is the contact force \(C\), so
\[
C=m_2a=(3.0)(3.0)=9.0\,\mathrm{N}.
\]

This example demonstrates why system choice is part of free-body-diagram skill,
not a separate topic.

\subsection*{Example 5: Atwood machine}

Masses \(m_1=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(m_2=5.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) are connected by an
ideal string over an ideal pulley.  The heavier mass moves downward.  The two
free-body diagrams give
\[
T-m_1g=m_1a
\]
and
\[
m_2g-T=m_2a.
\]
Adding gives
\[
a=\frac{m_2-m_1}{m_1+m_2}g
 =\frac{3}{7}(9.8)
 \approx4.2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
\]
Then
\[
T=m_1(g+a)
\approx2.0(14.0)
\approx28\,\mathrm{N}.
\]

\subsection*{Example 6: normal force at the bottom of a circular path}

A \(60\,\mathrm{kg}\) rider moves through the bottom of a circular arc of radius
\(20\,\mathrm{m}\) at \(10\,\mathrm{m/s}\).  Find the normal force exerted by the
seat on the rider.

At the bottom, the inward radial direction is upward.  The free-body diagram has
normal force upward and weight downward.  Therefore
\[
N-mg=m\frac{v^2}{r}.
\]
Thus
\[
N=m\left(g+\frac{v^2}{r}\right)
=60\left(9.8+\frac{100}{20}\right)
=888\,\mathrm{N}.
\]
The normal force exceeds the rider's weight because a net upward force is required
to bend the velocity vector toward the center of curvature.

\section*{Common errors}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Drawing motion as a force.} Velocity and acceleration are not forces.
They should not appear as force arrows on an inertial-frame free-body diagram.

\item \emph{Adding a centripetal-force arrow.} In an inertial frame, ``centripetal
force'' is the inward component of the net real force, not an extra interaction.

\item \emph{Assuming \(N=mg\).} The normal force is determined by the normal
equation of motion and may be larger or smaller than the weight.

\item \emph{Setting static friction automatically equal to \(\mu_sN\).} Static
friction adjusts up to its maximum magnitude.

\item \emph{Drawing both a force and its components as separate forces.} Choose one
representation.

\item \emph{Putting a Newton's-third-law pair on one body's diagram.} Third-law
partners act on different bodies.

\item \emph{Drawing forces exerted by the chosen object rather than forces acting
on it.} The free-body diagram represents the forces \emph{on} the system.

\item \emph{Including internal forces on a whole-system diagram.} Once several
bodies are selected as one system, forces between those bodies are internal.

\item \emph{Choosing awkward axes.} Coordinate axes should normally follow the
geometry of the constraints or acceleration.

\item \emph{Using a point diagram when torque matters.} A rigid-body diagram must
preserve force locations and lines of action when moments are needed.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{System-boundary strategy}

A free-body diagram is often easier if the system is selected to match the desired
unknown.

Suppose two bodies are connected by an ideal string.  If the goal is the common
acceleration, selecting both bodies as one system may eliminate the tension from
the equations.  If the goal is the tension, at least one of the bodies must then be
isolated after the acceleration is known.

The same idea applies to contact forces.  Internal contact forces cancel in the
sum of Newton's equations for the total system because of Newton's third law.  The
center-of-mass equation
\[
M\mathbf a_{\mathrm{cm}}=\sum\mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}
\]
is the general expression of this fact.

\section*{Free-body diagrams in non-inertial frames}

The usual free-body diagram contains real interaction forces and is used with
Newton's laws in an inertial frame.  In an accelerating reference frame, Newton's
second law can be retained in its familiar form only by introducing inertial or
fictitious forces.

For a frame translating with acceleration \(\mathbf a_0\) but not rotating, one
may write
\[
\sum\mathbf F_{\mathrm{real}}-m\mathbf a_0
=m\mathbf a_{\mathrm{relative}}.
\]
The term \(-m\mathbf a_0\) is not a new physical interaction; it is a consequence
of using a non-inertial coordinate system.

Rotating frames add centrifugal, Coriolis, and, when the rotation rate changes,
Euler inertial terms.  Such terms should be clearly marked as inertial forces so
that they are not confused with interactions such as gravity, contact, or tension.

\section*{Connection to analytical mechanics}

Free-body diagrams are central to Newtonian mechanics, but constrained systems can
become cumbersome when many unknown constraint forces must be included.  This is
one motivation for generalized coordinates and Lagrangian mechanics.

In Newtonian form, one writes all relevant external forces and then applies
\[
\sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a.
\]
In Lagrangian mechanics, ideal constraint forces can often be eliminated by
choosing coordinates that already satisfy the constraints.  The two methods are
not competing descriptions: the Lagrangian method reorganizes the same mechanics
so that some forces need not be solved explicitly.

The free-body diagram therefore remains valuable even after analytical mechanics
is introduced.  It clarifies which physical interactions exist, which forces are
constraints, and which generalized forces should appear in the equations.

\section*{Practice exercises}

Unless otherwise stated, use \(g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).  For each problem, draw a
free-body diagram before writing equations.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A \(4.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block rests on a horizontal table.  Find the normal
force.

\item A \(6.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block on a frictionless table is pulled horizontally
by \(18\,\mathrm{N}\).  Find its acceleration.

\item A \(10\,\mathrm{kg}\) block is pulled horizontally by \(40\,\mathrm{N}\)
on a surface with \(\mu_k=0.25\).  Find the acceleration.

\item A \(5.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block rests on a \(30^\circ\) frictionless incline.
Find the normal force and the component of gravity along the plane.

\item The block in the preceding problem is held at rest by a string parallel to
the incline.  Find the tension.

\item A \(3.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass hangs at rest from a vertical string.  Find the
tension.  Then state how the answer changes qualitatively if the mass accelerates
upward.

\item A \(2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block lies on top of a \(5.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block.
The floor under the lower block is frictionless.  A horizontal external force acts
on the lower block, and static friction prevents slipping between the blocks.
Draw separate free-body diagrams for each block and one diagram for the combined
system.

\item An elevator of mass \(800\,\mathrm{kg}\) accelerates upward at
\(1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).  Find the cable tension.

\item A \(1200\,\mathrm{kg}\) car travels at constant speed around a level circular
curve of radius \(50\,\mathrm{m}\) at \(15\,\mathrm{m/s}\).  Identify the force
that supplies the required radial acceleration and find its magnitude.

\item A \(0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass attached to a horizontal spring with
\(k=80\,\mathrm{N/m}\) is displaced \(0.10\,\mathrm{m}\) to the right on a
frictionless surface.  Draw the free-body diagram and find the initial
acceleration.

\item A skydiver of mass \(75\,\mathrm{kg}\) is falling at terminal speed.  Find
the magnitude of the drag force.

\item A \(2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block is pressed against a vertical wall by a
horizontal force of \(60\,\mathrm{N}\).  The coefficient of static friction is
\(0.40\).  Determine whether static friction can support the block.

\item Two masses \(m_1=4.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(m_2=6.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) form an
ideal Atwood machine.  Find the acceleration and string tension.

\item A \(50\,\mathrm{kg}\) rider passes over the top of a convex circular hill of
radius \(25\,\mathrm{m}\) at \(12\,\mathrm{m/s}\).  Find the normal force from
the seat on the rider.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \(N=39.2\,\mathrm{N}\).

\item \(a=3.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

\(a=1.55\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

\(mg\sin30^\circ=24.5\,\mathrm{N}\).

\item \(T=mg\sin30^\circ=24.5\,\mathrm{N}\).

\item At rest, \(T=mg=29.4\,\mathrm{N}\).  For upward acceleration, \(T>mg\).

\item The top block has weight, normal force, and horizontal static friction.  The

the upper block, the applied force, and friction opposite to that on the upper
block.  On the combined-system diagram, the inter-block normal and friction forces
are internal and are omitted.

\item \(T=m(g+a)=800(11.3)=9.04\times10^3\,\mathrm{N}\).

\item Static friction from the road supplies the radial force.  Its required
magnitude is \(mv^2/r=5.40\times10^3\,\mathrm{N}\).

\item The spring force is \(8.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the left, giving

\item At terminal speed, drag balances weight, so \(F_d=735\,\mathrm{N}\).

\item The required static friction is \(mg=19.6\,\mathrm{N}\).  The maximum is
\(\mu_sN=0.40(60)=24\,\mathrm{N}\), so the block can remain at rest.

\item

a=\frac{(6-4)g}{6+4}=1.96\,\mathrm{m/s^2},

and

Thus

\end{enumerate}

\section*{GRE-style speed practice}

The following questions are original practice questions designed to emphasize the
short conceptual and algebraic reasoning useful on a timed physics examination.
They are not reproduced from an ETS examination.

\begin{enumerate}
\item A block rests on a horizontal table.  Which two forces appear on the block's
free-body diagram if no other object touches it?

(A) weight and the block's force on the table

(B) weight and the table's normal force on the block

(C) the table's normal force and its Newton's-third-law partner

(D) weight only

(E) normal force only

\item A block slides down a rough incline.  Kinetic friction on the block points

(A) vertically upward,
(B) perpendicular to the plane,
(C) down the plane,
(D) up the plane,
(E) opposite gravity.

\item A box is pulled across a floor by a rope angled upward.  Compared with the
same horizontal pull magnitude, the upward rope angle generally

(A) increases the normal force,
(B) decreases the normal force,
(C) leaves the normal force unchanged,
(D) eliminates weight,
(E) reverses friction.

\item A car moves at constant speed around a level circular track.  In an inertial
frame, the free-body diagram should contain

(A) a separate inward centripetal force in addition to all real forces,
(B) no horizontal force,
(C) the real horizontal interaction that produces the inward net force,
(D) an outward centrifugal force only,
(E) an inward velocity vector as a force.

\item A mass hangs motionless from a single vertical ideal string.  Its tension is

(A) zero,
(B) less than \(mg\),
(C) equal to \(mg\),
(D) greater than \(mg\),
(E) impossible to determine.

\item A block is at rest on an incline and static friction is present.  Which
statement is always correct?

(A) \(f_s=\mu_sN\)

(B) \(f_s<0\)

(C) \(|f_s|\leq\mu_sN\)

(D) \(f_s=mg\)

(E) friction must point uphill regardless of other applied forces.

\item Two blocks are in contact on a frictionless horizontal table and are treated
as one system.  The contact force between them is

(A) an external force that must be included twice,
(B) an internal force and need not appear on the system free-body diagram,
(C) equal to the total weight,
(D) a fictitious force,
(E) zero.

\item At the bottom of a vertical circular path, a rider's speed is nonzero.  If
\(N\) is the upward normal force and \(mg\) is the downward weight, the radial
equation is

(A) \(N+mg=0\),
(B) \(mg-N=mv^2/r\),
(C) \(N-mg=mv^2/r\),
(D) \(N=mg\) always,
(E) \(N=mv^2/r\) with weight omitted.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Answers to GRE-style speed practice}

The answers are
\[
\boxed{1\text{ B},\;2\text{ D},\;3\text{ B},\;4\text{ C},\;5\text{ C},\;6\text{ C},\;7\text{ B},\;8\text{ C}}.
\]
