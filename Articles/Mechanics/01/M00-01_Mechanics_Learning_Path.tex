Classical mechanics is most useful when its subjects are understood as a connected
sequence of ideas rather than as a collection of unrelated formulas.  One first
learns how to describe motion, then how interactions change motion, and then how
energy, momentum, and angular momentum provide alternative descriptions of the
same dynamics.  These ideas extend naturally to gravitating systems, oscillators,
fluids, constrained systems, rigid bodies, and finally to Lagrangian and
Hamiltonian mechanics.

This entry gives a learning path through classical mechanics from a first
calculus-based college course to the principal topics of graduate classical
mechanics.  It is also useful as a checklist for review for the Physics Subject
Test of the Graduate Record Examination (GRE).  The order below is pedagogical:
it follows prerequisite relations rather than the order of a classification
scheme or a particular textbook.

A compact view of the progression is
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{kinematics}
\longrightarrow
\text{Newtonian dynamics}
\longrightarrow
\text{energy and momentum}
\longrightarrow
\text{rotation and systems of particles}
\\[3pt]
\longrightarrow
\text{gravitation, oscillations, and fluids}
\longrightarrow
\text{generalized coordinates and constraints}
\\[3pt]
\longrightarrow
\text{Lagrangian mechanics}
\longrightarrow
\text{Hamiltonian mechanics}
\longrightarrow
\text{advanced classical dynamics}.
\end{array}
\]

\section*{Mathematical preparation}

A student beginning calculus-based mechanics should be comfortable with algebra,
trigonometry, and elementary vector operations.  Calculus enters immediately
because velocity and acceleration are derivatives and because displacement,
impulse, and work are naturally expressed by integrals.

The most important mathematical prerequisites are:
\begin{itemize}
\item units, dimensions, and scientific notation;
\item scalar and vector quantities;
\item Cartesian vector components;
\item the dot product and cross product;
\item differentiation and integration of elementary functions;
\item elementary ordinary differential equations;
\item basic matrix algebra and eigenvalues for later work on normal modes and
rigid-body dynamics;
\item partial derivatives and multivariable calculus for analytical mechanics.
\end{itemize}

Dimensional analysis should be used throughout mechanics.  For example, if a
proposed expression for a time contains a term with dimensions of velocity, the
expression cannot be correct regardless of the numerical values inserted into it.
This kind of check is especially valuable in timed problem solving.

\section*{Readiness diagnostic}

The following questions locate a reasonable entry point into the path.

\subsection*{Preparation for introductory mechanics}

A student is ready to begin the introductory sequence if most of the following
statements are true.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A vector can be resolved into Cartesian components.
\item A scalar can be distinguished from a vector.
\item A derivative can be interpreted as a rate of change.
\item An integral can be interpreted as accumulated change.
\item Basic trigonometric relations can be used to resolve components.
\item Algebraic equations can be rearranged reliably for an unknown quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Preparation for analytical mechanics}

Before beginning Lagrangian mechanics, a student should normally be able to:
\begin{enumerate}
\item draw and use a free-body diagram;
\item decide when Newton's second law, energy conservation, or momentum
conservation is the most efficient method;
\item solve elementary collision problems;
\item compute or use a moment of inertia;
\item work with torque and angular momentum as vectors;
\item solve the simple harmonic oscillator differential equation;
\item use partial derivatives and elementary multivariable functions;
\item carry out basic matrix operations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Preparation for graduate mechanics}

A student entering graduate classical mechanics should additionally be
comfortable with generalized coordinates, Lagrange's equations, multivariable
Taylor expansions, linear algebra for coupled differential equations, vector
calculus, coordinate transformations, and first-order systems of differential
equations.

\section*{Stage I: kinematics}

\emph{Kinematics} describes motion without yet specifying the interactions that
produce it.  The basic hierarchy is
\[
\mathbf v=\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt},
\qquad
\mathbf a=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}
          =\frac{d^2\mathbf r}{dt^2}.
\]

A complete introductory treatment should include position and displacement,
average and instantaneous velocity, average and instantaneous acceleration,
motion graphs, constant acceleration, variable acceleration, projectile motion,
uniform circular motion, and relative motion.

The constant-acceleration formulas should be understood as results of integration,
not as independent formulas to memorize.  If \(\mathbf a\) is constant, then
\[
\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf v_0+\mathbf a t
\]
and
\[
\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_0 t+\frac12\mathbf a t^2.
\]

At a more advanced level, kinematics should be repeated in polar, cylindrical,
and spherical coordinates.  The derivatives of the coordinate basis vectors then
contribute to velocity and acceleration, preparing the student for central-force
motion and rotating reference frames.

\paragraph{Checkpoint.}
Given \(\mathbf r(t)\), the student should be able to obtain velocity and
acceleration, interpret their directions, and describe the resulting motion.

\section*{Stage II: Newtonian dynamics}

Dynamics connects motion to physical interactions.  Newton's second law may be
written in the momentum form
\[
\sum \mathbf F=\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt},
\]
which becomes
\[
\sum \mathbf F=m\mathbf a
\]
for a particle of constant mass.

The central skill in elementary dynamics is converting a physical description
into a force model.  This requires mastery of free-body diagrams and of common
forces such as weight, normal force, tension, static and kinetic friction, spring
forces, and drag forces.  Connected bodies, pulleys, inclined planes, and curved
paths provide important examples because they require careful choices of system
boundary and coordinate axes.

Circular motion is especially important because constant speed does not imply
zero acceleration.  For uniform circular motion,
\[
a_r=\frac{v^2}{r}=\omega^2 r,
\]
directed toward the center of curvature.

\paragraph{Checkpoint.}
Given a mechanical configuration, the student should be able to isolate an
appropriate body or system, draw a free-body diagram, select useful axes, and
write the corresponding equations of motion.

\section*{Stage III: work, energy, and potential}

Newton's laws give a local description of dynamics through acceleration.  Work
and energy often provide a more direct global description between two states.
For a particle acted on by a force \(\mathbf F\),
\[
W_{1\to2}=\int_1^2 \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
\]
The net work satisfies the work--energy theorem
\[
W_{\mathrm{net}}=\Delta T,
\]
where the translational kinetic energy is
\[
T=\frac12 mv^2.
\]

For a conservative force, a potential energy \(U\) may be introduced so that
\[
\mathbf F=-\nabla U.
\]
In one dimension,
\[
F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx}.
\]
When only conservative forces do work, the mechanical energy
\[
E=T+U
\]
is constant.

Potential-energy diagrams introduce turning points and stability.  An equilibrium
position \(q_0\) satisfies
\[
\left.\frac{dU}{dq}\right|_{q_0}=0,
\]
and a one-dimensional equilibrium is stable when
\[
\left.\frac{d^2U}{dq^2}\right|_{q_0}>0.
\]
This local quadratic structure later becomes the basis of small-oscillation
theory.

\paragraph{Checkpoint.}
The student should be able to decide whether a problem is best solved by force
balance, the work--energy theorem, or conservation of mechanical energy.

\section*{Stage IV: momentum and systems of particles}

Linear momentum is
\[
\mathbf p=m\mathbf v
\]
for a particle of constant mass.  Integrating Newton's second law over time gives
the impulse--momentum relation
\[
\mathbf J=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F\,dt
          =\Delta\mathbf p.
\]

For an isolated system, total linear momentum is conserved.  This principle is
the natural tool for collisions and explosions.  Both one- and two-dimensional
collisions should be studied, including elastic and inelastic limits.

For a system of particles of total mass \(M\), the center of mass is
\[
\mathbf R=\frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i,
\]
with the continuous-distribution analogue obtained by replacing the sum by an
integral.  The motion of the center of mass obeys
\[
M\ddot{\mathbf R}=\sum \mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}.
\]
This is the first important demonstration that an appropriate system boundary can
eliminate internal forces from the equations of motion.

\section*{Stage V: elementary rotational mechanics}

Fixed-axis rotation parallels translational mechanics.  The angular variables
\(\theta\), \(\omega\), and \(\alpha\) correspond to position, velocity, and
acceleration.  Torque is
\[
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F,
\]
and for a rigid body rotating about a fixed principal axis,
\[
\sum\tau=I\alpha.
\]

The moment of inertia is the rotational measure of mass distribution,
\[
I=\int r_\perp^2\,dm.
\]
The rotational kinetic energy about a fixed axis is
\[
T_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac12 I\omega^2.
\]

Angular momentum is
\[
\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p
\]
for a particle, and torque gives its rate of change,
\[
\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}=\boldsymbol\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}.
\]
Rolling without slipping combines translation and rotation through the
constraint
\[
v_{\mathrm{cm}}=R\omega.
\]

This stage should include the parallel-axis theorem, rotational work and power,
static equilibrium, and conservation of angular momentum.

\paragraph{Checkpoint.}
The student should be able to move fluently between translational and rotational
analogues and to recognize when angular momentum is the simplest conserved
quantity.

\section*{Stage VI: gravitation and orbital mechanics}

Newtonian gravitation provides the first major application in which energy,
angular momentum, and central forces work together.  For point masses,
\[
\mathbf F=-\frac{G M m}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r},
\]
and the gravitational potential energy is
\[
U(r)=-\frac{G M m}{r}.
\]

Introductory study should include the gravitational field, escape speed, circular
orbits, orbital energy, and Kepler's laws.  A circular orbit about a mass \(M\)
has speed
\[
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
\]
and period
\[
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}
\]
when the orbiting mass is negligible compared with \(M\).

At the analytical-mechanics level, the two-body problem is reduced to one body
with the reduced mass
\[
\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.
\]
Conservation of angular momentum confines central-force motion to a plane and
leads naturally to the effective-potential method and the orbit equation.

\section*{Stage VII: oscillations}

The simple harmonic oscillator is a prototype for linear dynamics.  For
\[
m\ddot x+kx=0,
\]
the angular frequency is
\[
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},
\]
and the motion may be written
\[
x(t)=A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi).
\]

The introductory sequence should include oscillator energy, the simple pendulum,
damping, forced motion, resonance, phase, bandwidth, and the quality factor.
The next step is coupled oscillation and normal modes.

Near a stable equilibrium, many nonlinear systems can be approximated by a
quadratic potential.  This is why harmonic motion appears throughout mechanics,
molecular physics, structures, waves, and field theory.

\section*{Stage VIII: elementary fluid mechanics and elasticity}

Elementary fluids are part of a complete undergraduate mechanics foundation.
The sequence should include density, pressure, hydrostatic equilibrium, Pascal's
principle, Archimedes' principle, the continuity equation, Bernoulli's equation,
viscosity, and elementary pipe flow.

For a static fluid of density \(\rho\) in a uniform gravitational field,
\[
\frac{dp}{dz}=-\rho g.
\]
For steady incompressible flow through a stream tube,
\[
A_1v_1=A_2v_2.
\]
Along a streamline for an ideal incompressible fluid under the usual Bernoulli
assumptions,
\[
p+\frac12\rho v^2+\rho gz=\text{constant}.
\]

Elasticity provides a bridge from particle mechanics to continuum mechanics
through stress, strain, elastic moduli, and elastic energy.

\section*{Stage IX: generalized coordinates and constraints}

Analytical mechanics begins by asking what coordinates actually describe the
independent motion of the system.  A set of generalized coordinates
\(q_1,\ldots,q_n\) specifies the configuration using the number of independent
variables required by the constraints.

Important distinctions include holonomic and nonholonomic constraints and
scleronomic and rheonomic constraints.  Virtual displacements and d'Alembert's
principle explain how ideal constraint forces can often be eliminated from the
equations.

The transition from Cartesian coordinates to generalized coordinates is not
merely a change of notation.  It is a systematic way to build the geometry of the
constraints into the description of the system.

\section*{Stage X: variational and Lagrangian mechanics}

For many mechanical systems the Lagrangian is
\[
L(q_i,\dot q_i,t)=T-U.
\]
The action is the functional
\[
S[q]=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q_i,\dot q_i,t)\,dt.
\]
Hamilton's principle states that the physical path makes the action stationary
with respect to admissible variations whose endpoints are fixed.  The resulting
Euler--Lagrange equations are
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\right)
-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.
\]

A complete upper-undergraduate treatment should include generalized forces,
Lagrange multipliers, generalized momentum,
\[
p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i},
\]
cyclic coordinates, first integrals, and the relation between continuous
symmetries and conservation laws expressed by Noether's theorem.

\paragraph{Checkpoint.}
Given a constrained mechanical system, the student should be able to select useful
generalized coordinates, construct \(T\) and \(U\), form \(L=T-U\), and derive
the equations of motion.

\section*{Stage XI: non-inertial reference frames}

The derivative of a vector depends on the rotating frame in which it is
measured.  If a frame rotates with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), then
\[
\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{I}
=
\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{R}
+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A.
\]
Applying this relation to position and velocity produces the centrifugal,
Coriolis, and Euler acceleration terms.  This material is important both for
advanced particle dynamics and for rigid-body mechanics.

Applications include motion on the rotating Earth, the Foucault pendulum,
rotating machinery, and navigation.

\section*{Stage XII: central forces and scattering}

For a central potential \(U(r)\), conservation of angular momentum reduces the
radial dynamics to motion in an effective potential,
\[
U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)
=U(r)+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.
\]
This representation reveals radial turning points and the stability of circular
orbits.

The central-force sequence should continue through the orbit equation, the Kepler
problem, the Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector as an advanced symmetry, and classical
scattering.  Scattering introduces the impact parameter, scattering angle, and
differential cross section.

\section*{Stage XIII: small oscillations and normal modes}

For small displacements \(\boldsymbol\eta\) about stable equilibrium, a broad
class of systems reduces to
\[
\mathbf M\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta}
+\mathbf K\boldsymbol\eta=0,
\]
where \(\mathbf M\) is the mass matrix and \(\mathbf K\) is the stiffness matrix.
Seeking harmonic solutions gives the generalized eigenvalue problem
\[
\det\left(\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M\right)=0.
\]
The eigenvectors are the normal modes and the corresponding eigenvalues determine
the normal frequencies.

This subject connects ordinary oscillators to molecular vibration, structural
dynamics, lattice models, and the continuum limit.

\section*{Stage XIV: three-dimensional rigid-body dynamics}

A rigid body has both translational and rotational degrees of freedom.  The
advanced rotational sequence is
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{rotation representation}
\longrightarrow
\boldsymbol\omega
\longrightarrow
\mathbf I
\longrightarrow
\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega
\\[3pt]
\longrightarrow
\text{Euler equations}
\longrightarrow
\text{precession and nutation}.
\end{array}
\]

Direction cosine matrices, Euler angles, axis--angle coordinates, and quaternions
are alternative representations of attitude.  They should be studied after the
physical meaning of torque, angular momentum, and inertia has been established.

The inertia tensor is diagonal in principal-axis coordinates.  In a body-fixed
principal-axis frame, Euler's equations relate applied torque to the components of
angular velocity.  Torque-free motion, the intermediate-axis instability,
symmetric tops, precession, and nutation form the standard advanced rigid-body
sequence.

\section*{Stage XV: Hamiltonian mechanics}

The canonical momentum is
\[
p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}.
\]
When the Legendre transformation is regular, the Hamiltonian is
\[
H(q_i,p_i,t)=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L.
\]
Hamilton's equations are
\[
\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},
\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}.
\]

The conceptual transition is from second-order dynamics in configuration space to
first-order dynamics in phase space.  For a system with \(n\) degrees of freedom,
phase space has \(2n\) canonical coordinates \((q_i,p_i)\).

The next topics are Poisson brackets, constants of motion, canonical variables,
Liouville's theorem, and the symplectic structure of Hamiltonian flow.  For
functions \(f(q,p,t)\) and \(g(q,p,t)\), the Poisson bracket is
\[
\{f,g\}
=\sum_i\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}
-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}
\right).
\]

\section*{Stage XVI: graduate classical dynamics}

A graduate sequence at approximately the level of a standard advanced mechanics
text should proceed from Hamiltonian mechanics into the following subjects.

\subsection*{Canonical transformations}

Canonical transformations change the phase-space variables while preserving
Hamilton's form of the equations.  Generating functions provide a systematic
method for constructing them.  Infinitesimal canonical transformations connect
naturally to Poisson brackets and symmetry generators.

\subsection*{Hamilton--Jacobi theory}

The Hamilton--Jacobi equation is
\[
H\left(q_i,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i},t\right)
+\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}=0.
\]
It reframes mechanics as a first-order partial differential equation and provides
an important bridge to geometrical optics and quantum mechanics.

\subsection*{Action--angle variables}

For integrable periodic systems, action--angle variables make the Hamiltonian a
function of the action variables alone.  They provide a natural language for
fundamental frequencies, adiabatic invariants, and perturbation theory.

\subsection*{Perturbation theory and nonlinear dynamics}

Near-integrable Hamiltonian systems lead to canonical perturbation theory,
averaging, and secular terms.  More general nonlinear systems introduce phase
portraits, fixed points, stability, bifurcations, Poincare sections, and
deterministic chaos.

\subsection*{Continuous systems and fields}

The variational formulation extends from finitely many coordinates to fields.  A
field action has the form
\[
S=\int \mathcal L(\phi,\partial_\mu\phi,x)\,d^4x,
\]
and the field Euler--Lagrange equations provide a bridge to strings, elastic
continua, fluids, and classical field theory.

\section*{GRE Physics mechanics checklist}

The GRE Physics Subject Test treats classical mechanics as a substantial part of
the examination.  A mechanics review should include at least the following
subjects:
\begin{itemize}
\item kinematics;
\item Newton's laws;
\item work and energy;
\item oscillatory motion;
\item fixed-axis rotational motion;
\item systems of particles and conservation of momentum;
\item central forces and celestial mechanics;
\item three-dimensional particle dynamics;
\item introductory Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics;
\item non-inertial reference frames;
\item elementary fluid dynamics.
\end{itemize}

For timed preparation, each subject should be practiced at several levels:
conceptual recognition, one- or two-step calculation, scaling and dimensional
analysis, conservation-law shortcuts, graph interpretation, and limiting-case
reasoning.  Long derivations are essential for learning, but they should be
supplemented by short problems that train rapid identification of the governing
principle.

\section*{A dependency map in words}

The main prerequisite relations can be summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Vectors and calculus lead to kinematics.
\item Kinematics and force modeling lead to Newtonian dynamics.
\item Newtonian dynamics leads to work--energy methods and momentum methods.
\item Energy and momentum together lead naturally to rotational mechanics and
systems of particles.
\item Rotation and energy support gravitation, orbital mechanics, and elementary
oscillation theory.
\item Newtonian mechanics plus coordinate methods lead to generalized coordinates
and constraints.
\item Generalized coordinates plus variational calculus lead to Lagrangian
mechanics.
\item Lagrangian mechanics supports non-inertial dynamics, central-force theory,
small oscillations, and advanced rigid-body dynamics.
\item A Legendre transformation of a regular Lagrangian leads to Hamiltonian
mechanics.
\item Hamiltonian mechanics leads to Poisson brackets, canonical transformations,
Hamilton--Jacobi theory, action--angle variables, perturbation theory, and modern
nonlinear dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Recommended study method}

A useful mechanics study cycle has five parts.

\paragraph{Physical model.}
Identify the system, degrees of freedom, interactions, reference frame,
constraints, and likely conservation laws before manipulating equations.

\paragraph{Derivation.}
Work through the origin of the governing equation.  Equations such as Newton's
second law, the work--energy theorem, Bernoulli's equation, the Euler--Lagrange
equations, and Hamilton's equations should not be treated as unrelated formulas.

\paragraph{Worked examples.}
Reproduce a worked example without looking at intermediate steps, and then change
one assumption to test whether the method is understood rather than memorized.

\paragraph{Exercises.}
Use a mixture of conceptual, computational, derivational, and numerical problems.
Computation becomes increasingly valuable for nonlinear oscillation, orbital
mechanics, coupled systems, and rigid-body dynamics.

\paragraph{Timed review.}
For examination preparation, add short timed problems that emphasize recognition,
scaling, conservation laws, dimensions, and limiting cases.

Progress should be judged by the ability to set up an unfamiliar problem, not by
the number of pages read.

\section*{Milestones}

\subsection*{Introductory particle mechanics}

A student completing this level should be able to translate motion among words,
graphs, and equations; draw correct free-body diagrams; solve Newton's-law
problems in one and two dimensions; use energy and momentum appropriately; and
analyze collisions and center-of-mass motion.

\subsection*{Introductory rotation, gravitation, oscillation, and fluids}

A student completing this level should be able to solve fixed-axis rotational
dynamics, use torque and angular momentum, analyze rolling, solve elementary
orbit problems, solve simple damping and resonance problems, and use hydrostatics,
continuity, and Bernoulli's equation.

\subsection*{Analytical mechanics}

A student completing this level should be able to identify degrees of freedom and
constraints, select generalized coordinates, derive Lagrange's equations, use
cyclic coordinates and generalized momentum, and analyze representative central
force, non-inertial, normal-mode, and rigid-body problems.

\subsection*{Hamiltonian mechanics}

A student completing this level should be able to construct a Hamiltonian, use
Hamilton's equations, work in phase space, compute basic Poisson brackets, and
explain the structural meaning of phase-space volume conservation.

\subsection*{Graduate mechanics}

A student completing the advanced path should be able to use canonical
transformations and generating functions, solve representative Hamilton--Jacobi
problems, use action--angle variables, understand perturbative and nonlinear
departures from integrability, and connect discrete mechanics to continuum and
field formulations.
